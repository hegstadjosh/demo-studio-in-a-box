# Treatment

Decide what the film is while a change costs a paragraph, not a render, so the person can react early and later drafts can be tested against the treatment. Write `treatment.md`, a `storyboard/` folder and `research.md`, then record the chosen premise and its runner-up in the decision ledger of `project.md`. When the person supplied the premise, record it as given and make only parts 5 to 7 and the storyboard.

## `treatment.md`

**1. Viewer question.** What the viewer can answer at the end, and the change you want in them, in the terms of the goal in `project.md`. Thesis, story and marketing goal are separate choices; the thesis can stay unstated, assembled by the viewer from the beats' order.

**2. Decision-space sample.** Purpose, lens or genre, situation, cast, topic and beats are separate axes: a heist-film frame, a parent and a teenager, a family group chat and the question of who finished the milk can coexist. List a few values per axis, titled as a sample, not a menu.

**3. Rival premises.** At least two, each with the real fragment it rests on, what it asks the viewer to believe or feel, its hook, the material it needs and its specific risks.

*Real fragment.* Recent, recognizable real material, such as a public figure's provocative quote, can supply a hook, a question and a way into the subject at once; in the source production one, proposed by the person, reorganized the film. Whether agents find one unaided is untested. On a tightly specified instructional brief a topical hook distracts; rest the premise on the brief's task.

*Beat selection.* Choose content for the audience's interest, not for coverage, because interest makes a viewer act; in the source production a competent feature summary missed its promotional purpose. This fails when the request is a feature overview.

**4. Genre conventions, as candidates.** List what a professional edit of this genre and platform contains: narrator, music, titles, contest rounds, posting norms. Their absence can read as amateur, yet a device can break the flow: in the source production the person asked for narration and music, then cut the rounds. Test each in the storyboard or a bare stitch before polish.

**5. Beat sheet.** For each beat: its job, what the viewer knows before and after it, and a device wherever a viewer may leave. When a beat feels arbitrary, ask what purpose its section serves, why this example suits it, and what must be set up before its payoff lands.

A recurring situation, character, rivalry or question can spare repeated setup and let later scenes develop what the viewer knows. **Hypothesis:** in a promotional product film, showing what distinguishes parallel features holds interest that a repeated setup spends; it fails in a tutorial, where a repeated setup teaches the pattern.

**Hypothesis:** give each beat the screen time its job needs, because a viewer reads a beat only as far as its job requires. A fast grid of many examples can show a platform's breadth without any card being read, while the main reply or a needed explanation must stay long enough to be understood; a closing invitation fits the breadth shown before it, since an invitation wider than the film asks the viewer to trust what was not shown. This came from the source production's product films and is not established elsewhere. It fails when the grid's items are themselves the claim, such as names the viewer must recognize; then show fewer, longer.

*Narration's job.* Ask what the picture leaves unclear: narration can connect a compelling scene to the product's larger meaning, or interrupt a self-explanatory exchange and repeat visible names, whatever the duration.

**6. Whole-film dimensions.** Intended treatment of each: story, edit, interface, every kind of sound, music, narration, transitions, pacing, muted viewing. Where one is absent, write "not used" and why; `seeds/audit.md` walks this list on every full draft.

**7. Text checks** for every label, caption and line of copy, run by the audit. Judge every line by its role: orientation, premise, response, transition, invitation, or deliberate humour. **Hypothesis:** the audience's own words land faster than a coined tagline, which the viewer must decode before reacting; in the source production on-screen comedy came from timing and plain words, and written jokes fell flat. Not established for brand films built on a slogan. It fails when the plain phrase is a cliché; then the coined line may earn its decoding. Keep a provocation the person chose; it is the reason to watch.

**Must (truthfulness):** a quoted provocation is quoted accurately and attributed.

## `storyboard/`

One rough frame per beat, captioned with its job and built cheaply from coded stills, footage frames or a contact sheet, so order and look are judged before any render. Show it through `tools/show`.

**Optional deck.** When the premise or the audience's starting knowledge is unclear, a short, mostly verbal idea deck may surface the audience's mental model before the structure is set; its value in the source production is not established. The deck's order need not become the film's, since a deck spells out reasoning that a short film can leave to its beats.

## `research.md`

In parallel with other work, research the product's real features, the real discourse the film draws on, and earlier films of the genre and their narration.

When only a recording is supplied, it is the source of truth for features and brand: sample the logo, colours and copy from its frames, and search the web for the on-screen product name only to confirm, since a page may be stale or about another product.

**Hypothesis:** an on-screen comment grounded in a real one reads as real discourse, where an invented one reads as generic; in the source production invented comments improved once each carried a real argument or a joke that made one.

**Must (truthfulness):** every source is dated and attributed; a synthesis is labelled as yours; every authored comment, quote or example is marked authored in the truth list.

## Presenting and verifying

In the source production, where the person answered quickly, numbered premises led by a recommendation drew one-word decisions. Keep the runner-up recoverable, because the person's taste governs and your recommendation is a guess at it.

**Must (explicit requirement):** when the person has said not to involve them, ask nothing. Choose the premise by the viewer question and record the choice as yours; where the web conflicts with a supplied recording, prefer the recording and record the conflict in `research.md`.

Before showing the treatment, check that each premise's fragment is in `research.md`, each beat has a frame and each dimension a treatment or reason. Then read the beat sheet as a viewer who knows only the viewer question: it should show what the film is, why to keep watching and what each beat adds.
